# Basics of antimicrobial resistance gene annotation

*A note on scope:* most of the detailed reference material available for this section is about antimicrobial resistance (AMR) gene detection specifically, using ABRicate and AMRFinderPlus. General structural and functional annotation (gene calling, product naming, RNA genes, and so on) is covered below with Bakta, but only at the level of what it does and why it's used here — deeper detail on its specific options isn't available in the source material for this module.

## This training's scenario: a carbapenem-resistant outbreak

At this point in the training you have a polished bacterial genome assembly, built from long reads — you're no longer dealing with long-read-specific accuracy problems, and the question shifts to what you can actually learn from a finished genome. This module works through one of the most common real-world uses of bacterial long-read sequencing: identifying antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes, in the context of the outbreak investigation introduced earlier in this training.

The scenario is a hospital-acquired outbreak of infections resistant to carbapenems — a "last resort" class of broad-spectrum antibiotics, reserved for the most severe, multi-drug-resistant cases, and part of the wider beta-lactam (*bla*) drug class. The genes most clinically significant for this scenario, and the ones you should be specifically looking out for in your assemblies, are:

- ***bla*KPC** — *Klebsiella pneumoniae* carbapenemase
- ***bla*IMP** — Imipenemase-type metallo-β-lactamase
- ***bla*VIM** — Verona integron-encoded metallo-β-lactamase
- ***bla*NDM** — New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase
- ***bla*OXA** — Oxacillinase-48

AMR genes like these can sit either on the main bacterial chromosome or on a plasmid — a distinction that matters a great deal here. Plasmids are mobile: they can be passed between bacterial cells, and even between species, independently of normal reproduction. An AMR gene carried on a plasmid is therefore far more capable of spreading through a hospital environment than one fixed on the chromosome, which makes plasmid-borne carbapenemase genes a particular concern for outbreak control.

## Why antimicrobial resistance matters

Antimicrobial resistance occurs when bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites stop responding to the medicines used to treat them, making infections harder — sometimes impossible — to treat. It's a major global health threat: bacterial AMR was associated with more than 4.7 million deaths globally in 2021, and roughly 1 in 6 laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections worldwide were resistant to antibiotics in 2023. Between 2018 and 2023, resistance rose in over 40% of the pathogen–antibiotic combinations being monitored, at an average annual increase of 5–15%. Resistance isn't evenly distributed either — it's currently highest in the WHO South-East Asia and Eastern Mediterranean regions, where around 1 in 3 reported infections are resistant, and tends to be worse wherever health systems lack the capacity to diagnose or treat bacterial infections properly. Among drug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria, *E. coli* and *K. pneumoniae* are the leading causes of resistant bloodstream infections — among the most severe bacterial infections there are, often progressing to sepsis, organ failure, and death.

Misuse and overuse of antibiotics is one of the main drivers. In 2022, "access" antibiotics — the recommended first-choice treatments for most common infections — accounted for only about 53% of global human antibiotic use, short of the global target of at least 70% by 2030; "watch" antibiotics, which carry a higher resistance risk, made up around 45% of use and exceeded 70% of total use in nearly a third of countries. The knock-on effects reach well beyond the clinic: AMR drives up healthcare costs through longer hospital stays and greater reliance on intensive care, second-line antibiotics, and additional diagnostic testing, and it disproportionately affects vulnerable populations while also threatening food security through its impact on animal and plant health.

## How bacteria become resistant

Resistance isn't a single mechanism — it's a whole toolkit bacteria can draw on, and it's worth understanding the basic biology before interpreting a resistance gene report. Whenever a population of bacteria is exposed to an antibiotic, any cells that happen to be resistant gain a survival advantage: as susceptible bacteria die off, the resistant ones multiply and come to dominate the population. Widespread antibiotic use is, in effect, a selection pressure for resistance.

Broadly, bacteria resist antibiotics in one of two ways: by preventing the antibiotic reaching its target at an effective concentration, or by modifying or bypassing the target itself.

**Keeping the antibiotic away from its target:**
- **Efflux pumps** — membrane- or cell-wall-embedded pumps that can expel a variety of compounds, including antibiotics, out of the cell. Mutations that cause a bacterium to overproduce a particular pump can increase resistance further.
- **Reduced membrane permeability** — changes to the bacterial membrane that make it harder for the antibiotic to get in at all.
- **Destroying the antibiotic** — bacterial enzymes that inactivate the drug outright. β-lactamase, which breaks down the β-lactam ring in penicillins, is the classic example; extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing bacteria, which can degrade a much broader range of β-lactam antibiotics — including some last-resort options — have become a major clinical problem.
- **Modifying the antibiotic** — enzymes that attach chemical groups to the antibiotic, preventing it from binding its target.

**Modifying or bypassing the target itself:**
- **Camouflaging the target** — mutations or chemical modifications that change the target's structure enough to stop the antibiotic recognising it.
- **Expressing alternative proteins** — producing a substitute protein that does the same job as the one the antibiotic inhibits. *Staphylococcus aureus* acquiring the *mecA* gene, which produces an alternative penicillin-binding protein with low affinity for β-lactam antibiotics, is the basis of MRSA (methicillin-resistant *S. aureus*).
- **Reprogramming the target** — building a structurally different version of something the antibiotic needs to bind. Vancomycin-resistant bacteria, for example, construct a different cell wall that the antibiotic can no longer interact with effectively.

Some bacteria are naturally immune to a given antibiotic from the outset — intrinsic resistance, such as an antibiotic that targets a cell wall structure a particular species simply doesn't have. Acquired resistance is different: it's when a previously susceptible population evolves resistance over time, whether through spontaneous mutation or by picking up resistance genes from elsewhere.

That second route matters a great deal for genomic surveillance: resistance genes are often carried on mobilisable genetic elements such as plasmids, which allows horizontal gene transfer to spread resistance rapidly through — and between — bacterial populations, independent of normal reproduction. Carbapenem resistance is a well-documented example of this in action: carbapenems are broad-spectrum antibiotics used for a wide range of infections, and over the last three decades, resistance has spread worldwide via an expanding array of carbapenemase genes, driving outbreaks of carbapenemase-producing bacteria in healthcare settings globally.

## Annotation tools

### General annotation with Bakta

Before drilling into AMR genes specifically, it's worth knowing where that search normally fits within a wider genome annotation. Bakta is a general-purpose bacterial genome annotation tool: it bundles together a range of underlying tools and databases to annotate a genome assembly for a broad set of features in one pass, including RNA genes (tRNA, rRNA, ncRNA, and CRISPR arrays) and coding sequences and protein-coding genes across the genome. Usefully for this module, Bakta also wraps AMRFinderPlus internally, so a full Bakta run can surface AMR gene calls as part of its standard output, alongside the rest of the genome's annotated features.

This training's AMR-focused sections use ABRicate and AMRFinderPlus directly, since that gives more visibility into how the AMR gene search actually works — but in a real annotation workflow, running Bakta first to get a complete, general-purpose annotation of the genome, with AMR gene detection included, is a very reasonable starting point.

### AMR gene detection with ABRicate and AMRFinderPlus

To screen your assembled contigs for AMR genes, this training uses ABRicate configured to search against the NCBI Bacterial Antimicrobial Resistance Gene Database, via AMRFinderPlus, selected from the tool's advanced options. For each hit, the report tells you whether an AMR gene was found, which contig it's on, its location within that contig, the specific product name, and what substance it provides resistance against, along with further supporting detail.

Under the hood, AMRFinderPlus compares your isolate's genome against a curated reference protein set using BLAST, and separately against a curated HMM set using HMMER, then uses a gene hierarchy to assign the most specific protein or gene family it can justify from the evidence. This matters more than it might sound: unlike tools that simply report the closest database hit, AMRFinderPlus is built to avoid over-claiming. If it encounters a novel *bla*KPC allele that's nearly identical to *bla*KPC-2, a closest-hit approach might mislabel it *bla*KPC-2 outright — AMRFinderPlus instead calls it *bla*KPC, avoiding the implication that it necessarily behaves exactly like the specific *bla*KPC-2 variant. Beyond core AMR genes, AMRFinderPlus also identifies select virulence factors and biocide, heat, acid, and metal resistance genes.

Once you have a hit, it's worth checking which contig it landed on and, from your assembly output, whether that contig corresponds to the main chromosome or a plasmid — for a carbapenemase gene in particular, that distinction is exactly what determines whether you're looking at a fixed, single-lineage resistance marker or a mobile element with the potential to spread to other bacteria in the same ward.

### Core vs Plus: what's actually being screened

AMRFinderPlus splits its reference database into two subsets. By default, only the "Core" subset is searched: highly curated, AMR-specific genes and point mutations drawn from the Bacterial Antimicrobial Resistance Reference Gene Database, built from allele assignments, cross-checks against other curated resources, and reports of novel resistance proteins in the literature. The "Plus" subset — genes related to biocide and stress resistance, general efflux, virulence, or antigenicity, or AMR genes whose phenotypic effect is uncertain or unlikely — is only included if you explicitly enable the `--plus` option.

### Making sense of the "Method" column

The "Method" column tells you how confident a given hit is, and it's worth learning to read at a glance:

- **X / P / N suffixes** — indicate what kind of sequence the hit came from: translated nucleotide (X), protein (P), or nucleotide sequence directly (N — used specifically for non-coding mutations, such as those in 16S rRNA or promoter regions).
- **EXACTX / EXACTP / ALLELEX / ALLELEP** — the strongest hits: an exact amino-acid match to a database entry. ALLELE is used specifically for gene families with a formal allele-numbering scheme, so the reported "gene symbol" in that case is really an allele designation for that exact sequence.
- **BLASTX / BLASTP** — hits below 100% identity but above the coverage cutoff (by default 90% identity, though this can be manually curated higher or lower for particular genes).
- **PARTIALX / PARTIALP / PARTIAL_CONTIG_ENDX / PARTIAL_CONTIG_ENDP** — hits covering less than 90% of the reference protein's length. "PARTIAL_CONTIG_END" specifically flags cases where the alignment runs off the very end of a contig, in an orientation suggesting the gene continues beyond the assembly boundary — in other words, the gene is probably intact in reality, but got split by a fragmented assembly rather than being genuinely partial. This is a useful reminder that assembly contiguity (the whole reason long reads are worth the effort in this training) directly affects how cleanly your AMR results come out. By default, hits covering under 50% of the reference aren't reported at all, so a PARTIAL hit covers somewhere between 50% and 90%.
- **INTERNAL_STOP** — a premature stop codon appears when the hit is translated, making the gene unlikely to be functional. This can only be assessed when nucleotide sequence is available as input.
- **HMM** — hits that don't clear the BLAST threshold but do clear a curated HMM cutoff for that gene family — typically distant relatives of known resistance genes, and occasionally candidates for entirely new gene families.
- **POINTX / POINTP / POINTN** — flag detected point mutations, only searched for when a specific `--organism` option is supplied. Because AMRFinderPlus only reports mutations with documented functional relevance in the literature, the absence of a POINT result doesn't rule out a functional mutation being present — it may simply not be in the database yet. The `--mutation_all` option outputs every genotype call made, including ones that didn't turn up a hit, which is useful if you need to confirm a specific gene was checked for at all.

## Genotype is not phenotype

This is worth stating plainly: finding an AMR gene in your assembly does not, by itself, mean the isolate is actually resistant to the corresponding antibiotic. AMRFinderPlus identifies genes and mutations with the *capacity* to contribute to resistance — it does not predict resistance phenotype directly. A gene has to actually be expressed to confer resistance, and many AMR proteins only reduce antibiotic susceptibility partially, not enough to cross a clinically meaningful resistance threshold. The reverse can happen too: an isolate can gain or lose resistance through mechanisms with no corresponding gene hit at all, such as losing the porin protein an antibiotic needs to enter the cell in the first place.

Some gene families — particularly plasmid-borne ones — do show a much clearer, more reliable genotype-to-phenotype relationship than others, but even then, over-interpretation is a real risk. In one validation exercise, AMRFinder's predicted genotypes were checked against measured resistance phenotypes across 6,242 isolates in the National Antimicrobial Resistance Monitoring System, and showed strong consistency for a set of four foodborne pathogens specifically — but that level of agreement isn't guaranteed to carry over to other species. In practice, treat an AMR gene hit as a strong lead worth following up, not as a confirmed resistance result on its own.
