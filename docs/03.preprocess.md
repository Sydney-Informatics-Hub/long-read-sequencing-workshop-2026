# Basics of long read preprocessing

Once QC has told you what's actually in your reads, the next question is what — if anything — to do about it. For long reads, that's a genuinely open question rather than a formality: unlike short-read QC, which more or less requires trimming as a matter of course, long-read QC tools have traditionally just displayed the picture and left filtering and trimming as an optional next step, not a mandatory one.

## Does long-read data need the same preprocessing as short reads?

Short-read pipelines are built around the assumption that you'll trim adapters and quality-trim ends before anything else happens. Long-read tooling has historically taken a lighter touch: the QC step shows you the read length and quality distributions, and it's up to you whether filtering is worth doing at all, rather than assuming it's needed. 

## To preprocess or not to preprocess?

For bacterial isolate assembly with a modern long-read assembler, the honest answer is: often, not much. Flye, the assembler used in this training, is explicit about this — its own documentation states that preprocessing usually isn't necessary, since Flye automatically filters out chimeric reads and reads with bad ends, and adapter trimming and quality filtering aren't required either. Oxford Nanopore gives essentially the same guidance from the sequencing side: most of their own benchmarks are built on the default pass/fail cut-offs applied in MinKNOW, so pass-filtered reads straight out of basecalling should generally already be fit for analysis, without a dedicated trimming step in between.

That's not a blanket rule, though — it's a reasonable default, not a law. Whether preprocessing is worth doing still depends on your aim (raw contiguity vs consensus accuracy), the specific quality of your data (error rate, depth, length, how quality varies across a read), and which assembly tool you're using, since not every assembler handles messy ends and adapters as gracefully as Flye does. 

## What to check before deciding

Before deciding whether to filter, it's worth actually looking at what your QC reports are telling you: average read length, average quality, and whether quality varies meaningfully across the length of a read. For long reads specifically, the NanoPlot "read length vs average read quality" plot is the one to focus on — for example, a nanopore run might show a mean read quality sitting around Q9, which by itself isn't a red flag so much as a data point to weigh against your read depth. If you have plenty of reads to spare, it's reasonable to set a stricter quality threshold and filter more aggressively; if reads are scarcer, a lighter touch — or no filtering at all — may serve you better, since throwing away marginal reads costs you coverage you might actually need.

## Coverage and read length requirements for assembly

Coverage and read length both matter more than any single quality metric when it comes to whether an assembly will actually close. As a rough guide, 30x or greater coverage of PacBio or ONT reads, with an N50 of several kilobases, is generally enough for a contiguous assembly; higher coverage still can help improve consensus quality on top of that. Coverage in the 20–30x range can still work reasonably well depending on the platform and the read length distribution, but datasets below 10x coverage generally aren't worth attempting to assemble. Flye specifically expects coverage above 10x and an N90 read length over 1 kb (5 kb or more is preferable), and it simply won't run on reads shorter than 1 kb.

## Filtering and trimming tools

If your QC results do point towards filtering or trimming — noisy reads, a long tail of very short fragments, adapter contamination, or simply more reads than you need — a few tools are commonly used for long-read data. In this tutorial we will use Filtlong and fastplong.

### Filtlong

Filtlong filters long reads using a combination of read length (longer is better) and read identity (higher is better), producing a smaller, better subset of your original reads. Without an external reference, it judges quality from the Phred scores already in your FASTQ file. A typical command looks like:

```
filtlong --min_length 1kb --keep_percent 90 --target_bases 500mb input.fastq.gz | gzip > output.fastq.gz
```

Here, `--min_length` discards anything shorter than the given threshold, `--keep_percent` throws out the worst-scoring reads by base count (not by read count) — so `--keep_percent 90` discards the worst 10% of bases — and `--target_bases` trims the read set down until only the given amount of sequence remains, which is handy for very large datasets.

Under the hood, each read gets a length score (a 5 kb read is considered middling and scores 50, with longer reads scoring higher), a mean quality score (scaled relative to the rest of the read set), and a window quality score (based on the read's single weakest stretch, using a sliding window). The final score combines length and mean quality as a weighted geometric mean, then gets scaled down according to window quality — so a read that's long and generally high-quality but has one bad patch will still be penalised for that patch. The relative importance of length versus quality can be tuned with `--length_weight` and `--mean_q_weight`: pushing `--length_weight` up favours longer reads even at some cost to identity, while pushing `--mean_q_weight` up favours cleaner reads.

### fastplong

fastplong is a fast, general-purpose preprocessing and QC tool for long reads (Nanopore, PacBio, and others), built along similar lines to the popular short-read tool fastp. It supports quality filtering (discarding reads below a mean quality or with too high a proportion of low-quality bases), length filtering (minimum and maximum read length), GC content filtering, and N-base filtering, all enabled by sensible defaults and each individually switchable off if you don't want it.

It also handles adapter trimming — either using known adapter sequences you supply, or by auto-detecting likely adapters at the start and end of reads — and offers finer-grained options for cases where blanket filtering isn't precise enough: sliding-window quality trimming from the front and/or tail of a read, splitting a read into separate "subreads" around low-quality internal regions, masking low-quality stretches with N rather than removing them outright, and simple global trimming of a fixed number of bases from either end (useful, for instance, for dropping a consistently low-quality final sequencing cycle). For processing large datasets in parallel, fastplong can also split its output across multiple files.
