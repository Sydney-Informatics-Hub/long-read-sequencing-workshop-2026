# Basics of long read quality assessment

Every sequencing platform introduces errors — incorrect bases called, reads that are too short to be useful, occasional contaminating DNA that has nothing to do with your isolate. Left unchecked, these issues bias downstream analysis and can lead to a genome assembly that looks plausible but is subtly wrong. Quality control is therefore always the first real step of analysis, not an optional add-on.

In this training, the QC module covers a short pipeline: assess read quality before and after preprocessing using NanoPlot, FastQC, and MultiQC; trim and filter reads by length and quality using Porechop and Fastp; and remove host sequences before assembly. The isolates you'll work with in this training are bacterial cultures recovered from patients as part of a hospital outbreak investigation into carbapenem-resistant infections — but even from a pure culture, some host or environmental DNA can carry over into an extraction, so the pipeline screens for it regardless: Minimap2 checks specifically for chicken (*Gallus gallus*) sequence, and Kraken2 (with the Kalamari database and Krakentools) checks more broadly for other likely contaminants, such as human or cow DNA, before the non-host reads move on to assembly.

## Why do we assess quality?

Sequencing errors are a technical limitation of the platform itself, not a flaw in your sample — but if they're not identified and dealt with, they bias the analysis and can lead to real misinterpretation of the data. Before starting any analysis, it's always worth assessing the quality of your input data and discarding poor-quality content by trimming and filtering reads. For bacterial isolate assembly specifically, this matters even more than usual: a handful of low-quality or contaminating reads can be enough to fragment an otherwise-closable genome or introduce a spurious variant into your consensus sequence.

## How do we assess quality?

For short reads, quality assessment typically examines read length, GC content, per-base quality scores, sequence complexity, duplication levels, ambiguous bases, artefacts, and contamination. This meticulous assessment ensures that only high-quality, reliable data carries through to further analysis. Long-read sequencing (LRS) needs a different QC approach: read lengths are far broader and more variable than in short-read data, which calls for its own set of metrics rather than a straight reuse of short-read QC tools.

Two metrics come up constantly across both short- and long-read QC, and are worth understanding properly before looking at any actual reports: the Phred quality score, and read N50.

### Phred score

The Phred score is a measure of how confident the base-calling algorithm is in each individual base call. It's logarithmic, so small changes in the score represent large changes in expected accuracy:

| Phred quality score | Probability of incorrect base call | Base call accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 1 in 10 | 90% |
| 20 | 1 in 100 | 99% |
| 30 | 1 in 1,000 | 99.9% |
| 40 | 1 in 10,000 | 99.99% |
| 50 | 1 in 100,000 | 99.999% |
| 60 | 1 in 1,000,000 | 99.9999% |

A Phred score of 20 (Q20) is a common baseline threshold — it means, on average, only 1 in 100 called bases is expected to be wrong.

### Read N50

N50 is the read length at which the cumulative length of all reads at or above that length equals 50% of the total bases in the dataset. Put differently: half of all the sequenced bases in your run sit inside reads that are at least this long. For example, if a run's N50 is reported as 5,974 bp, that means half the total sequence generated is contained in reads of 5,974 bp or longer — not that most individual reads are that length.

N50 is especially informative for long-read data, where read lengths vary widely and a simple mean can be misleading. We'll come back to N50 again in the assembly module, where it becomes just as important as a measure of assembly contiguity as it is here as a measure of raw read length.

## Short reads vs long reads quality metrics

Short-read and long-read QC tools were built around different assumptions, and it shows. FastQC — built for short reads — doesn't report read- or base-level mapping QC, and it's missing long-read-relevant metrics such as mean, median, maximum, and N50 read length; it also hasn't been optimised to handle the large file sizes typical of long-read runs. Going the other way, long-read-focused QC tools tend to lack features that short-read users take for granted, such as adapter searching, overrepresented sequence analysis, and duplication analysis.

There's also a subtler difference worth knowing about: some long-read QC tools work directly from the sequencing summary file rather than the FASTQ itself, and by default may not calculate quality scores against the expected error rate the way short-read best practice recommends. In practice, this can mean the average quality reported is a slight overestimate — worth keeping in mind rather than taking the headline number at face value.

*A note on error rates:* the source material for this section flags that nanopore reads have a higher raw error rate than Illumina short reads, and that this gap continues to close with each Dorado release, but doesn't specify current figures here. For up-to-date numbers, see the accuracy discussion in the introduction — current R10.4.1 chemistry with modern basecallers reaches greater than 99% single-read accuracy for simplex reads, and over 99.9% for duplex reads.

For per-sequence quality distributions specifically, expect the shape to differ by platform too: for Nanopore data, the peak is generally somewhere around Q10–12, though exactly where depends on the pore chemistry (R9 vs R10) and which basecalling model was used (Fast, HAC, or SUP).

## Software overview

A range of tools exist to pull basic statistics out of a sequencing run — read length distribution, presence of adapter sequence, contamination from small fragments — and to filter out low-quality, too-short, or adapter-contaminated reads before they reach assembly. Because this training works with Oxford Nanopore long-read data, NanoPlot is the tool we'll lean on most heavily. FastQC and MultiQC are still worth knowing, since they run on ONT data too and cover some ground NanoPlot doesn't, but we'll treat them as a secondary, complementary check rather than the main event.

### NanoPlot

NanoPlot is part of NanoPack, a widely used QC suite built specifically for long-read sequencing data. NanoPack bundles several sub-tools — NanoPlot, NanoQC, Cramino, and others — covering quality assessment and general data analysis for long reads. The more recent NanoPack2 release builds on this with code optimisations, more plotting options, and dynamic HTML plots in both NanoPlot and NanoComp.

#### Why NanoPlot is the right tool for long reads

NanoPlot reports the long-read-specific statistics that FastQC simply doesn't provide — mean, median, maximum, and N50 read length — and it's designed to cope with the large file sizes long-read runs typically produce, which FastQC was never optimised for.

That said, it isn't a strict upgrade in every respect. Tools in this space that work from the sequencing summary file rather than the reads themselves don't report on sequence composition, sequence content, or duplication profile — information FastQC does provide. So in practice, the two tools tell you different things, and for a thorough picture of a long-read dataset it's worth glancing at both rather than treating either as a complete replacement for the other — which is exactly why FastQC still gets a (brief) look later in this module.

### FastQC

FastQC takes FASTA or FASTQ files as input and produces a comprehensive quality report. It displays sequence quality, sequence content, GC content, N content (ambiguous bases), length distribution, and duplication levels, and also flags overrepresented sequences and analyses k-mer content. Together, these let you spot problems in a run at a glance and decide how to handle them.

After QC, data is typically filtered on length, GC content, quality score, sequence count, and similar criteria, and reads can be trimmed to a defined length. Trimming should always happen before filtering, not after — doing it in the wrong order can introduce duplication artefacts and leave you filtering a dataset that doesn't reflect your actual trimmed reads.

Beyond FastQC and NanoPlot, it's worth knowing that pycoQC and nanoQC exist as alternatives: pycoQC in particular produces a comprehensive interactive HTML report covering read quality, length, coverage, active channels, and quality over the course of the run.

## Results interpretation

The next sections work through what each report actually shows, starting with NanoPlot's long-read-specific views, then a condensed look at FastQC output aggregated through MultiQC.

### NanoPlot output

NanoPlot can be run directly on FASTQ (or aligned BAM) files rather than the sequencing summary alone, which gives it access to per-read sequence information rather than just summary statistics — this is the primary way we'll be assessing long-read QC in this training.

#### Summary statistics

Because it works from the actual reads, NanoPlot reports the long-read-specific figures that FastQC leaves out: mean, median, and maximum read length, and N50.

#### Histogram of read length (weighted/non-weighted, log-transformed)

This plot shows the distribution of read (fragment) lengths within a sample. Unlike most Illumina runs, where read length is essentially fixed by the sequencing chemistry, long reads vary considerably in length, so this histogram shows the relative amount of sequence at each different fragment size. *The reference material doesn't detail the specific difference between the weighted, non-weighted, and log-transformed variants of this plot — worth checking the NanoPlot documentation directly if you need the precise definitions.*

#### Read lengths vs average read quality dot plot/kde plot

This plot combines read length and read quality (Q-score) into a single view, plotting one against the other. In general, there's no strong relationship between read length and read quality, but visualising them together in one plot makes it easy to spot aberrations. One pattern that does show up reasonably often: in runs containing a lot of short reads, those shorter reads are sometimes of noticeably lower quality than the rest of the run.

#### NanoPlot from summary data

When NanoPlot is run against the sequencing summary file instead of the FASTQ/BAM reads themselves, it's working with less information: it can still report on read quality, read length, and channel activity over the course of the run, but — because it never sees the actual sequence — it can't report on sequence composition, sequence content, or duplication profile the way a FASTQ-based tool can.

### FastQC via MultiQC

Rather than opening a separate FastQC report for every sample, MultiQC aggregates FastQC output from all your samples into a single, comparable summary report. FastQC's report is built around a dozen individual panels; grouped by what they're actually checking for, they boil down to four things worth knowing how to read.

#### Read counts and length distribution

Two panels are largely bookkeeping checks: total sequence counts per sample — useful for spotting a sample with unexpectedly few reads before you invest time interpreting the rest of its report — and the overall read length distribution within a sample. *The reference material for this section doesn't go into further detail on either panel; for short-read platforms, length distribution is normally a narrow peak around the expected read length, and a wide or multimodal shape is generally worth investigating.*

#### Quality score profile

It's common for mean quality to drop off towards the end of reads, since sequencers tend to incorporate more incorrect bases as a read progresses — but a very large, sudden drop partway through a read is a sign something's gone wrong rather than a fact of life. Two mechanisms specific to Illumina-style sequencing-by-synthesis explain the more gradual version of this pattern:

- **Signal decay** — fluorescent signal intensity decreases with every sequencing cycle as fluorophores degrade, so a growing proportion of strands in a cluster stop being extended. As emitted signal weakens, quality scores drop towards the 3′ end of the read.
- **Phasing** — as cycles progress, some strands in a cluster randomly fail to incorporate a nucleotide correctly, whether from incomplete removal of previous 3′ terminators and fluorophores or from incorporation of nucleotides lacking an effective terminator. The cluster gradually loses synchronicity, blurring the signal and again dragging quality scores down towards the 3′ end.

Looking at quality per sequence rather than per position, the distribution should ideally form a tight peak in the upper range of the plot. A secondary cluster of low-quality sequences can appear too — often because those particular reads were poorly imaged (for example, sitting at the edge of the imaging field of view) — but this should only ever account for a small percentage of the total.

#### Base and GC content

Per-base sequence content plots the percentage of each of the four bases (A, C, G, T) at every position across all reads. In a random, unbiased library, there should be little to no difference between the four bases at any given position — %A should track %T, and %G should track %C — so the four lines on the plot should run roughly parallel to each other throughout the read.

Per-sequence GC content plots the number of reads against the percentage of G and C bases in each read, compared against a theoretical distribution assuming uniform GC content across all reads — as expected for whole-genome shotgun sequencing. Since the true GC content of the genome isn't known in advance, the modal GC content from the observed data is used to build that reference distribution. An unusually shaped distribution can indicate a contaminated library or another biased subset of reads. A distribution that's shifted but still roughly normal in shape points to a systematic bias rather than contamination — but because the tool doesn't know your genome's expected GC content in advance, this kind of shift won't necessarily be flagged as an error on its own. For long-read data, this distribution carries even more weight, since it's also shaped by fragment size selection, primer choice, and the amount and quality of input DNA — all worth checking if the GC plot looks off for one of your isolates.

A related panel, per-base N content, tracks the percentage of ambiguous "N" calls at each read position. *The reference material doesn't provide much specific detail here beyond noting that a sharp spike usually points to a problem with the sequencer or basecaller at that particular position.*

#### Duplication and contamination

In a diverse library, most sequences should occur only once in the final dataset. A low level of duplication can simply indicate very high coverage of the target sequence, but a high level of duplication is more likely to point to some kind of enrichment bias. One major source of duplication is PCR amplification, where particular library fragments become over-represented through biased PCR enrichment — a real concern, since PCR duplicates misrepresent the true proportions of sequences in your input. Duplication profiles are useful for both PCR-based and PCR-free library preparations, since the expected rate of natural read duplication differs between DNA and RNA experiments.

A related check flags individual sequences that make up an outsized share of a sample: a normal, diverse library shouldn't have any single sequence making up more than a tiny fraction of the total, so finding one that does means either it's genuinely, biologically significant, or the library is contaminated or less diverse than expected. FastQC specifically lists every sequence making up more than 0.1% of a sample's reads, and for each one searches a database of common contaminants for the closest match, requiring at least a 20 bp match with no more than one mismatch — a hit doesn't automatically confirm the source of contamination (many adapter sequences closely resemble one another) but is still a useful lead.

Finally, adapter content: multiplex identifiers, adapters, and primer sequences introduced during library preparation show up as artefacts at the ends of reads. An uneven base frequency distribution at certain positions across many reads can be a sign of residual tag sequence — not necessarily a problem in itself, but it does mean those sequences will need to be adapter-trimmed before downstream analysis. FastQC plots a cumulative percentage of the library in which each known adapter sequence has been seen, by position — since a read is counted as "containing" an adapter for the rest of its length once the adapter is first detected, this percentage can only increase as you move along the read.

## Sequencing bias

Oxford Nanopore sequencing doesn't rely on a polymerase to generate sequence data. Instead, library preparation attaches a motor protein to an adapter sequence, using either ligation-based chemistry (the ligation sequencing kit) or transposase-based chemistry (the rapid sequencing kit). Both approaches involve enzymatic steps that can introduce motif bias — ligation-based methods tend to under-represent adenine-thymine (AT)-rich sequences, and transposase-based methods carry their own insertion biases.

Several specific mechanisms contribute to this during library preparation:

- **PCR amplification** is a major cause of uneven sequencing coverage in regions of extreme GC content.
- **Tn5 transposase** has an insertion preference that can produce lower sequencing depth in GC-poor regions.
- **MuA transposase** shows its own insertion bias, again contributing to imbalanced coverage.
- **Elevated incubation temperatures** during sample and library preparation can reduce coverage of high-AT regions.

Left uncorrected, this biased coverage can cause real downstream problems: loss of genuine single-nucleotide variants, a misrepresented picture of microbial composition in mixed samples, and misinterpreted results in analyses sensitive to coverage evenness. Being aware of where these biases come from — and which library prep kit you used — makes it much easier to recognise when an odd-looking coverage or GC plot reflects a real biological signal versus an artefact of the chemistry.
